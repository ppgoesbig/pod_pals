# Learning
Okay, something new today, and it's right in your lane. How a camera sensor actually sees color, because it mostly doesn't.

Each pixel on a sensor only measures how much light hits it. Brightness, nothing else. To get color, there's a tiny grid of colored filters laid over the top. Red, green, or blue, one filter per pixel. It's called a Bayer pattern, and it repeats in little squares of four. One red, one blue, and two greens.

Why two greens? Your eyes pick up most of their detail from green light. So the sensor spends twice as much on it.

That means every single pixel only knows one color. A red pixel has no idea how much green or blue landed on it. So the camera guesses. It looks at the neighbors and fills in the two missing colors for every pixel. That step is called demosaicing. Two-thirds of the color in every photo you take is an educated guess.

Usually the guess is great. Where it breaks is fine repeating detail. A tight weave, a grille, finely engraved silver. The pattern gets close to the size of the filter grid, the guess gets confused, and you see rainbow shimmer that isn't there. That's moire.

A raw file stores the mosaic before the guess. That's partly why two raw editors can render the same file a little differently.

And here's the trick for still product work. Some cameras have a pixel shift mode. The sensor nudges itself one pixel at a time between shots, so every spot gets measured in red, green, and blue for real. No guessing, cleaner color, and the shimmer on fine detail mostly goes away. It only works when nothing moves, which a piece of silverware on a table is great at.
