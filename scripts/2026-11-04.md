# Learning
Okay, something new today, and it's one for the bike. Why more air in your tires isn't always faster.

Most of the drag from a tire comes from the tire itself bending. Every time a bit of the tire rolls onto the ground, it squashes flat, then springs back as it rolls away. The rubber and the casing don't give all that energy back. Some of it turns into a little bit of heat. That's rolling resistance.

So on a perfectly smooth floor, harder is faster. More air means less squash, which means less energy lost. That's where the old idea of pumping tires rock hard comes from.

But city streets aren't smooth. Over cracks, patches, and little bumps, a rock-hard tire can't swallow them. So the whole bike and your body get lifted and jolted instead. Your muscles and joints soak up that shaking, and that energy is gone too. Past a certain pressure, those losses grow faster than the tire's own losses shrink. So adding more air actually makes you slower, not just less comfortable.

Width plays into it too. At the same pressure, a wider tire touches the ground with about the same amount of rubber, but in a shorter, fatter patch. A short patch means less of the tire bends with each turn, so it rolls just as well, and you can run it softer.

The floor is a pinch flat. Go too soft, and a sharp edge like a pothole lip can squeeze the tube against the rim. So the sweet spot is just enough air to avoid that, and not much more. For rough city riding, that's usually lower than the max number printed on the side.
