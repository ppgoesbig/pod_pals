# Learning
Okay, something you've been learning. Remember setting up the memo's audio so every episode lands around minus sixteen? That number is the average loudness. But there's a second number in that same step, and it's about the peaks. Here's why it exists.

Digital audio is a row of dots. Each dot is one sample, a single height of the sound wave, taken tens of thousands of times a second. That's the raw P.C.M. you got back from the voice service. When the sound plays, your earbuds don't play dots. They draw a smooth wave through them.

Here's the catch. A smooth curve through a row of dots can swing higher than any single dot. Picture two tall dots side by side. The curve between them can bulge up above both. Those bulges are called inter-sample peaks. A meter that only checks the dots never sees them.

So a file can look safe, with no dot touching the top, and still clip when it plays. M.P.3 makes it worse. Remember how it throws away sounds you won't notice? Rebuilding the wave from what's left nudges its shape, and the peaks can creep up a little more.

That's why the setting is called true peak, not just peak. The meter guesses where the smooth wave will really go, not just where the dots sit. The common rule is to keep true peak under about minus one. That leaves a little headroom so nothing clips after conversion.

There's one more twist. If an episode comes in louder than an app's target, a lot of apps just turn it down. So pushing louder doesn't win you anything. You end up at the same level, just with squashed peaks.
