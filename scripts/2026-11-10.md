# Learning
Okay, something you've been learning. Remember finding out the voice service only hands back raw P.C.M.? Yesterday was about the dots in that file. Today's about the two numbers that decide how many dots there are, and why that file is so big.

The first number is the sample rate, how many dots per second. The rule is that you need at least two dots for every wave you want to catch. So to capture a sound up to some pitch, you sample at double it. People hear up to about twenty thousand cycles a second, which is why music uses about forty-four thousand samples a second. Speech doesn't need that much. The useful stuff in a voice sits lower, so a lot of voice services, including the one reading this, use twenty-four thousand. That still catches everything up to twelve thousand, which covers the crispness of an s or a t.

The second number is bit depth, how finely each dot's height is measured. Sixteen bits gives you about sixty-five thousand possible steps. That's fine enough that the rounding errors sit way below anything you'd hear on a run.

Now multiply. Twenty-four thousand dots a second, two bytes each, is about forty-eight thousand bytes every second. A five minute episode comes out around fourteen megabytes of raw audio. Turn that into an M.P.3 at a normal voice setting and it's closer to two or three. Same five minutes, about a sixth of the size, because the M.P.3 stores a description of the sound instead of every dot.

That's why the pipeline converts before publishing. Raw P.C.M. is perfect for editing and measuring. M.P.3 is what you send to a phone.
