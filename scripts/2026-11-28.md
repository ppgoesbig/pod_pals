# Learning
Okay, something to learn. You spend all day shooting silver, so here's what's actually going on with it.

Sterling silver is about ninety-two and a half percent silver, and the rest is mostly copper. That's why you see nine-twenty-five stamped on it. Pure silver is too soft to make a fork out of. It would bend in a steak. The copper stiffens it up.

Silver plate is something else. It's a cheaper base metal, like brass or nickel, with a really thin coat of silver put on electrically, thinner than a hair. It looks the same new. But on old pieces you can sometimes see the plate worn through, usually on the back of a spoon or the tips of fork tines, where it touches the table.

Now tarnish. Most people think it's rust, like oxygen eating the metal. It's not, really. Silver barely cares about oxygen. What it reacts with is sulfur. There are tiny amounts of sulfur gases in the air, plus more from eggs, onions, wool, rubber bands, even some cardboard. The sulfur bonds with the silver on the surface and makes silver sulfide, which is dark. Humidity speeds it up.

And here's a neat part. Tarnish goes yellow, then brown, then kind of blue and purple, before black. Those early colors aren't pigment. The film is so thin that light bouncing off the top and bottom of it interferes, like a soap bubble. That's why lightly tarnished silver can throw a warm cast in a photo even when it doesn't look dirty yet.

The fix is kind of fun too. Polish works by scrubbing off a tiny bit of silver along with the tarnish. But hot water, baking soda, and aluminum foil does it with chemistry. The sulfur jumps from the silver over to the aluminum, and the silver underneath goes back to being plain silver. Nothing's lost.
