# Learning
Okay, something new today, and it's a lighting one. Why color temperature numbers feel backwards.

On a camera, a low number like twenty-eight hundred Kelvin means warm, orange light, like an old bulb. A high number like sixty-five hundred means cool, blue light, like a cloudy sky. But in everyday talk, warm means hot. So why is the hot-sounding color the low number?

The scale comes from heating a chunk of black metal. As it gets hotter, it glows. First a dull red, then orange, then yellow, then white, and if you could push it far enough, a bluish white. Each color matches a real temperature in Kelvin. So the number is literally how hot that metal would be to give off that color. Blue light really is the hotter one. The art words, warm and cool, just run the other way.

Here's the part that trips people up on set. The white balance setting on your camera isn't describing your light. It's the correction. If you set the camera to twenty-eight hundred, you're telling it the light is orange, so it pushes everything toward blue to cancel it out. Set it too low under daylight, and the whole frame goes blue.

That's also why mixed light is a pain. A window at around fifty-five hundred and a lamp at around twenty-eight hundred can't both be neutral in one frame. You pick one to match, and the other one shifts color.
