# Learning
Okay, something new today, and it's right in your lane. Focus stacking, the trick behind those product shots where everything is sharp, front to back.

Here's the problem. Get close to something small, like a fork, and your depth of field gets razor thin. The tines are sharp and the handle melts away. Stopping way down helps, but only so far. Past about f eleven or so, most lenses start to soften everything a little. That's diffraction, light bending as it squeezes through the tiny opening.

So instead of one shot, you take a bunch. Camera locked on a tripod, nothing moving. You start with focus on the nearest edge, then nudge focus back a little each frame, until you reach the far end. Each frame has one thin slice that's sharp.

Then software lines them all up and, for every spot in the picture, keeps the pixels from whichever frame was sharpest there. It figures that out by looking for the most fine contrast, the crispest edges. Out comes one image that's sharp everywhere, at the aperture where your lens is at its best.

Two catches. First, focusing changes the framing slightly, so things grow a bit as you move focus. The software has to rescale each frame to match. Second, shiny stuff is hard. On silver, reflections move as focus shifts, so you can get halos or ghosty patches where a highlight lands in different spots. That's why a lot of people retouch those areas by hand.
