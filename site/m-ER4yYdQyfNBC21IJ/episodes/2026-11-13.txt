Okay, something you've been learning. Remember putting the memo feed behind a long random link? That link works like a password you can't change without changing the address. Today's about the two halves of that. Why guessing it is hopeless, and how it leaks anyway.

Start with the guessing. Say the random part is twenty-two letters and digits. Each spot has about sixty choices. Multiply that out and you get a number with about forty digits. Even a machine trying a billion links a second would need way longer than the universe has existed. Nobody's guessing it.

So the real risk is the link getting carried somewhere. Three quiet ways.

One, previews. Paste the link into a chat app, and the app's server visits it to build a preview card. Now a company you didn't think about has fetched it.

Two, logs and history. Your browser history, a synced browser on another device, and the server's own logs all keep the full address. Any of them can end up somewhere you didn't mean.

Three, directories. Some podcast apps pass the feeds people add along to public podcast directories. Add a private feed to one of those, and it can end up listed.

That's why the rule from before still holds. The link is only as secret as the least careful place it's been. If it shows up somewhere it shouldn't, swap in a new one.
