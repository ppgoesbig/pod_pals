Okay, something you've been learning. Remember those plywood wall shelves? Here's why plywood stays flat, and what actually decides how much a shelf sags.

Solid wood moves. It soaks up moisture from the air and swells, then dries and shrinks. Along the grain it barely changes at all. Across the grain it can move a few percent, which on a wide board is enough to cup, twist, or crack.

Plywood cheats that. It's thin sheets of wood glued up with the grain turned ninety degrees every layer. Each layer wants to swell sideways, and the next layer, running the other way, just won't let it. And there's almost always an odd number of layers, so the outside faces match and pull evenly. That's why a sheet stays flat.

Now the sag part. Two numbers matter way more than anything else, the span and the thickness. Sag goes up with the span to the fourth power. So if you double the distance between brackets, the shelf sags about sixteen times as much. Thickness works the other way, cubed. Double the thickness and it sags about an eighth as much.

Here's the catch with plywood. Since half its layers run crosswise, it's actually a little less stiff along its length than solid wood the same size. The classic fix is gluing a strip of solid wood along the front edge. It's like a tiny beam standing on its side, and it stiffens the shelf a lot for very little wood.

One last thing. Wood under a steady load keeps sagging slowly for months. That's called creep. Brackets a little closer together is the cheap insurance.
