Okay, something you've been learning. Remember printing parts for that iMac turned into a monitor? Here's what actually separates P.L.A. and P.E.T.G., and why one of them strings.

Start with glass transition. Plastic doesn't go straight from solid to liquid. Before it melts, there's a temperature where the long chains inside it loosen up, and the part goes from rigid to kind of rubbery. That's the glass transition. For P.L.A. it's low, around fifty-five to sixty Celsius. P.E.T.G. holds out until about eighty. So for anything sitting near warm electronics, like a screen with a backlight behind it, P.E.T.G. is the safer bet. P.L.A. can slowly sag there, even without ever melting.

Next, layer adhesion. A printed part is a stack of thin layers, and each new one has to weld onto the one below. That weld happens when hot chains from the new layer wiggle into the old layer before it cools. P.E.T.G. prints hotter and stays soft longer, so it fuses better. P.L.A. is stiffer, but more brittle. Either way, the joins between layers are the weak direction. So you want to orient a part so the load runs along the layers, not trying to peel them apart.

Now the stringing. That same soft, sticky quality is the catch. When the nozzle jumps across a gap, P.E.T.G. keeps oozing and pulls out thin hairs, like melted cheese. The fixes are pulling the filament back a little before each jump, printing a few degrees cooler, and keeping the spool dry. P.E.T.G. soaks up moisture from the air, and that water boils in the nozzle, which makes the oozing worse.

So, short version. P.L.A. is easy and stiff, but it hates heat. P.E.T.G. is tougher and handles heat, but it's messier to print.
