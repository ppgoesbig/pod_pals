Okay, something new today, from making. It's about why 3D prints break where they do.

A printer builds a part one thin layer at a time. Inside a single layer, the plastic is laid down as one continuous bead, so it's strong along that line. But each new layer only bonds to the one below while it's still hot. That seam between layers is never quite as solid as the plastic itself.

So a printed part has a grain, a little like wood. Pull along the layers and it holds up. Pull them apart, and it can snap at a fraction of the load. Depending on the plastic and the settings, the weak direction can be half as strong or worse.

That means the way you lay a part on the bed matters as much as the design. Picture a small hook. Print it standing up, and the hanging weight pries the layers apart right at the bend. That's exactly where it cracks. Lay it flat on its side, and the layers run around the curve, so the load follows the grain instead.

A few things help the layers bond better. Running the nozzle a bit hotter, slowing down, and cutting back on the cooling fan for the parts that need strength. Thicker walls usually help more than adding infill, too.

So before you hit print on anything that'll carry a load, picture how it'll get pulled. Then turn it so the layers aren't the thing holding on.
