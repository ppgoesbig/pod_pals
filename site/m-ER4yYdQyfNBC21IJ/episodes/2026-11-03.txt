Start with the guessing. A good random link carries about a hundred and twenty-eight bits of randomness. That's around twenty-two characters of jumble. The number of possible links is a three with thirty-eight zeros after it. Say someone could try a billion links every second. Running through them all would take something like a trillion times longer than the universe has existed. Even being lucky only gets you so far. So a long random link really can't be guessed. A short one, or one built from a pattern like a date or a name, is a different story.

Now the leaks, and a few of them are sneaky.

The first is link previews. Paste a link into most chat apps and the app's servers go fetch it, just to draw that little preview card. Nobody clicked, but a computer you don't control has now seen it.

The second is the trail a click leaves. When you tap a link on a page, your browser can tell the next site where you came from. Modern browsers mostly trim that down to just the site's name, but older setups and some apps still pass along the whole address.

The third is being linked from anywhere public. If the link ever lands on a public page, search engines can find it and list it. There's a polite request you can add to a page asking search engines to stay away, but it's a request, not a wall.

And the last one is the big one. You can't take a link back. Once it's out, there's no unsending it. The only fix is changing the lock. That means making a fresh random link, moving the thing there, and killing the old one. Anyone you still want in gets the new link.

So the honest version is this. An unguessable link is a strong lock with no key log. It's great for things where a leak would be a little awkward. For things where a leak would really hurt, you want a real login, so you can see who's coming in and shut one person out without changing it for everyone.
