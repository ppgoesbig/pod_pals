Okay, something you've been learning. Remember noticing that to-dos left out in plain sight don't actually get done any faster? There's a name for that. Habituation.

Habituation is about the simplest kind of learning there is. When something keeps showing up and nothing happens, your brain slowly stops responding to it. The ticking clock, the hum of the fridge, the sticky note on the monitor. It isn't that you stop seeing them. Your brain has just filed them under safe, nothing to do here.

The classic proof came from a sea slug. Poke it gently and it pulls its gill in. Keep poking and nothing bad happens, and it stops bothering. Researchers traced that to the actual connections between its nerve cells getting weaker with repetition. That work ended up winning a Nobel Prize. So it's not laziness, it's wiring.

And a few details make it useful. It's specific. You tune out that exact note in that exact spot, but change it, even a little, and it's back on your radar. That's called dishabituation. It also wears off with time away. Leave for a week and the clock ticks loud again. And it's different from your eyes or ears getting tired. A new sound still gets through right away.

So here's the practical bit. A reminder that never changes is going to fade, guaranteed. It works better if you move it, rewrite it, or tie it to a moment instead of a place. Something like, when I sit down with coffee, I do that one thing. A moment you hit once a day stays new in a way a note on the wall just can't.
