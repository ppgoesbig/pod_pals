Okay, something you've been learning. Remember setting up the memo's audio pipeline, and that target of minus sixteen LUFS? People just say luffs. Here's what that number is actually measuring.

There are two ways to set a level. The old way is peak normalization. You find the single loudest moment in the file and turn everything up until that moment touches the ceiling. The problem is that one cough or one plosive decides the volume of the whole episode.

Loudness normalization works on what your ear hears instead. It runs the audio through a filter that roughly matches human hearing, so deep bass counts for less and the upper mids count for more. Then it averages that across the whole file, and it ignores the silent gaps so pauses don't drag the number down. That average is the integrated loudness, in LUFS. One unit up or down is the same as one decibel.

Minus sixteen is the common target for podcasts on phones. TV broadcast in Europe sits much quieter, at minus twenty-three, because living rooms are quiet. Earbuds outside are not. At minus sixteen, a voice stays clear over traffic without you cranking it, and every episode lands at the same level, so you never reach for the volume between days.

The pipeline also keeps a true peak ceiling, around minus one, because turning audio into MP3 can push a peak a little higher than it was, and that's where clipping sneaks in.
